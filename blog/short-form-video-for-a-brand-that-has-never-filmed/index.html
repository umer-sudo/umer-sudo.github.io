Short-form video when you have never filmed

The object is the content, one idea per beat, captions for muted viewing, what a shoot day looks like, and what a month of eight videos comes to.

By Umer Tariq, founderPublished 22 October 20266 min read

Most brands that have never filmed think the obstacle is a camera or a presenter. It is neither. The obstacle is not knowing what the video is about, and the answer is almost always sitting on the shelf behind you.

The short version

Film the thing, not a person talking about the thing. Each video says one idea, in one beat. Write the captions as if the sound is off, because it is. A shoot day captures a month of material if the list is written first. Eight videos a month is a sustainable rhythm, and it is the one our Standard package is built around.

What is the video about?

The object is the content. The product, the room, the process, the thing in the case. A brand that sells towels films the towel being made, folded, used. A museum films the jacket. A café films the bread coming out. None of these need a presenter, a script or a studio. They need a phone, a window and a reason the object is interesting, which you already know, because you chose to sell it.

This is the lesson from the short films we make for Gettysburg Museum of History. The collection sits in glass cases on open display: uniforms, equipment, the belongings of the people who carried them. We film the piece, write one line, and cut it for a vertical screen. Who wore it, where, and why it sits behind this glass. A jacket with a name on it stops the scroll in a way that a person saying the museum is worth visiting never will.

Objects beat adjectives. Your product does not need to be a century old for that to be true. It needs a detail a viewer can look at, and a line that tells them what they are looking at.

Why one idea per beat?

A short video is not a shorter long video. It is one idea, delivered in one movement. The beat is the unit: a cut, a line, a reveal. A video that tries to say three things says none of them, because the viewer has decided whether to stay before the second one arrives.

In practice this means the first frame shows the object, the first line says the one thing, and the rest of the video earns the line. The overcoat he wore at the Bulge. The towel after a hundred washes. The dish that sells out by nine. One claim, then the proof of it, then stop. The ending is where most videos go wrong, because the maker keeps going after the idea has landed. Cut on the idea.

  • One object in frame, not the whole shop.
  • One line, written before the shoot, that the shot has to prove.
  • One movement: a reveal, a fold, a pour, a hand turning the piece over.
  • One ending, which is the moment the line is proven, and not a second later.

Why write captions for muted viewing?

Because the sound is off. People watch in queues, in bed, at work, on trains, and the default on most phones is silence. A video that depends on its audio is a video most viewers never understood. The caption is not an accessibility afterthought. It is the script.

So the one line goes on screen, in words, in the first second. Large, in the brand's own type, placed where the platform's interface will not cover it. If there is speech, it is captioned in full, and the captions are checked by a person, because the automatic ones get names and products wrong. If there is music, it is a bonus for the minority who have the sound on, and nothing depends on it.

A useful test before posting: watch the video on mute with the brightness low. If you could not say what it was about, nobody scrolling past will.

What happens on a shoot day?

A shoot day is a list, executed. Before anyone picks up a phone, there is a written shot list: the objects, the one line for each, the movement, the angle, the light. On the day, we work through the list in order, shooting more than we need of each so there is a choice in the edit. A well-planned day in one location produces a month of material. An unplanned day produces an afternoon of people wandering around with a camera.

What to have ready: the objects, clean and reachable; a window or a doorway for natural light; a table or a plain surface; the people who know the stories, for the lines we have not written yet. What not to do: tidy the place into something it is not. The workshop looks like a workshop. That is the content.

A shoot day on your premises is priced on its own, because the Standard package does not include on-site photography and videography; the pricing page says so in the exclusions rather than in a footnote. For a brand far from Karachi, the usual answer is that you film to our shot list on a phone, over a morning, and send us the footage. We write the list, we direct by message, we cut. The results are good because the list was good, not because the camera was.

What does a month of eight videos look like?

Eight short videos a month is the rhythm the Standard package is built around, alongside six carousels, four posters and the rest of the loop, at $4,000 a month. Eight is two a week, which is enough for the platform to keep showing you to new people and few enough that each one gets made properly. A brand posting daily with nothing to say trains its audience to skip it.

WeekThe two videosWhat they are for
OneThe object, plainly; the object in useIntroduce the thing to people who have never seen it
TwoThe process; the detailProve the claim: how it is made, what it is made of
ThreeThe question; the comparisonStart conversations and saves from people who are deciding
FourThe story; the month's best, recutGive the people who stayed a reason to come back

The recut in week four is deliberate. The video that performed best gets a new opening and goes out again. Most brands post a winner once and move on; the platform will happily show a good video to a new audience a second time if you let it.

What we report each month is what the videos did: reach, saves, how many people stayed past the opening, how many stayed to the end, and, where a video asked for something, whether people did it. Views alone are not in the report, because views do not pay invoices.

What will we not do?

We will not fake customer-made video. A paid actor pretending to have discovered your product in their kitchen is a lie the platform's audience has learned to spot, and it costs the brand more trust than it earns in clicks. If you want real customers on camera, we ask real customers, and some say no.

We will not chase a trend because it is a trend. A format that is everywhere this week is a format the audience is tired of by the time your version is edited. We use a format when it serves the object and not otherwise.

And we will not promise reach. The platform decides who sees a video, and nobody who sells short-form video can honestly tell you in advance how far one will travel. What we can promise is the method: the object, one idea, captions, a list, eight a month, and a report that says what happened. The first video will be the worst one. The eighth will be better. That is how it goes for everyone, including brands that film.
