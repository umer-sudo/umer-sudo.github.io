38:["$","h2","2",{"id":"checks","className":"disp","children":"What does a mail server check before it shows your email?"}]
39:["$","p","3",{"children":"When your message arrives at Gmail or Outlook, the receiving server looks up your domain and asks it a series of questions. Is the server that handed over this message one the domain says it uses? Does the message carry a signature that only the domain's real key could have made? Has the domain published a policy for what to do when those answers come back wrong? Only after that does it look at the content, the links and the history of how people have treated your mail before."}]
3a:["$","p","4",{"children":"Google and Yahoo now require bulk senders to pass these checks and to offer a one-click unsubscribe, and Microsoft has followed. A domain that skips them is not breaking a law. It is simply asking to be trusted without showing any identification, and the inbox treats it the way a bouncer treats a guest with no name on the list."}]
3b:["$","h2","5",{"id":"spf","className":"disp","children":"What is SPF, in plain words?"}]
3c:["$","p","6",{"children":"SPF, the Sender Policy Framework, is a single text record on your domain that lists the services allowed to send mail as you: your Google Workspace or Microsoft account, your newsletter tool, your outreach tool. If a message claims to come from your domain but arrives from a server not on the list, it fails."}]
3d:["$","p","7",{"children":"The common mistakes are small. A domain ends up with two SPF records instead of one, which counts as broken. A new sending tool is added without being added to the record. Or the record lists so many services, each of which lists more, that it passes the lookup limit receiving servers will follow. Each of those fails quietly. Nothing bounces. Your mail just starts arriving in junk."}]
3e:["$","h2","8",{"id":"dkim","className":"disp","children":"What is DKIM, and why is it the one people miss?"}]
3f:["$","p","9",{"children":"DKIM, DomainKeys Identified Mail, signs every message with a private key your mail provider holds, and publishes the matching public key on your domain. The receiving server checks the signature against the key. If it matches, the message really came through your provider and was not altered on the way."}]
40:["$","p","10",{"children":"It is the one people miss because nothing forces you to switch it on. Google Workspace sends mail perfectly well without it. You have to generate the key in the admin console, add it to your domain as a text record, and then press the button that starts signing. We found exactly this on our own main domain in October: the SPF record was right, a DMARC policy was in place and asking servers to quarantine anything that failed, and there was no DKIM key at all. So our own policy was telling Gmail to put our own mail in junk. We added the key the same day and the admin console now reports that it is authenticating."}]
41:["$","h2","11",{"id":"dmarc","className":"disp","children":"What does DMARC add?"}]
42:["$","p","12",{"children":"DMARC, Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance, is the rule that ties the other two together. It says what a receiving server should do when a message fails: nothing, send it to junk, or reject it. It also asks that the domain in the visible From line matches the domain that passed SPF or DKIM, which is what stops someone sending as you from a lookalike setup. And it names an address where servers send reports of what they saw."}]
43:["$","p","13",{"children":"Start with the policy set to none and read the reports for a couple of weeks. They will show you every service sending as your domain, including ones you forgot. Move to quarantine once your own mail passes, and to reject when you are sure. Point the reports at an inbox someone actually reads. Ours were going to an address at the old domain registrar that nobody checked, which is how a missing signature went unnoticed."}]
