How to get a museum on Google Maps

Claiming the listing, the category that matters, hours and events, photographs of the collection, and the reviews that bring visitors through the door.

By Umer Tariq, founderPublished 28 October 20264 min read

A visitor deciding what to do this weekend rarely starts on a museum's website. They type "museums near me" or the name of the town into Google, and the answer is a map with a few listings under it. If the museum is not there, or is there with the wrong hours, it has lost the visit before anyone read a word it wrote.

The short version

Claim the museum's Google Business Profile, verify it, set the primary category to Museum, and fill in everything: hours including holidays, the address, the website, the phone, photographs of the building and the collection. Post events as they come, answer every review, and keep it current. Google ranks local listings on relevance, distance and prominence, and a complete, active listing wins on the two a museum controls.

How do you add or claim a museum on Google Maps?

  1. Search for the museum on Google Maps. If a listing exists, open it and choose the option to claim or manage it. If not, add the place from Google Business Profile.
  2. Verify ownership. Google offers a method per business, often a short video of the building and the signage, sometimes a phone call or a postcard.
  3. Set the primary category to Museum, or the more exact one if it fits, such as History museum or Art museum. Add secondary categories only where true.
  4. Fill in the rest: hours, special hours for holidays and closures, the website, a phone number answered during opening hours, and accessibility details.
  5. Give the listing to an account the museum owns, with a second manager added, so it does not leave with one person.

What does Google use to rank a museum in Maps?

Google's own guidance names three things: relevance, meaning how well the listing matches the search; distance from the searcher; and prominence, meaning how well known the place is, which reviews, links and articles about it feed. A museum cannot move itself closer to a visitor, but it can make the listing complete and accurate, and it can earn reviews and mentions.

What should a museum post?

The collection, one object at a time, with a line on why it matters, and every event with its date. A post about a new exhibition, a lecture or an anniversary weekend gives the listing fresh activity and gives a searcher a reason to visit this week rather than someday. The same photographs and lines can feed the museum's social accounts; the object is the content everywhere.

How should a museum handle reviews?

Ask for them at the moment a visit goes well: a card at the desk, a line at the end of a tour. Never pay for them or trade them for a discount; Google removes those and can penalise the listing. Answer every review, the bad ones first and calmly. A reply that explains the parking or the closing time helps the next visitor more than the person who complained.

What have we done for a museum?

Our search work for the Gettysburg Museum of History in Pennsylvania included repairing the museum's listing, an event page for its October anniversary weekend and the Foundation's new site, written so people searching for the weekend landed on the museum's own page. No figure is published for it yet, and we would rather say so than invent one.

When does a museum not need outside help with this?

When someone on staff has an hour a week and will keep doing it. Claiming and filling in the listing is free and needs no agency. Outside help earns its fee when the listing is part of a larger push, such as an exhibition launch with films, posts and a page built to be found, or when nobody inside has the time.
