How to sell militaria online

Where to sell, how to write a listing a collector trusts, how to price from realised sales, and what changes between the US, the Netherlands and the rest of Europe.

By Umer Tariq, founderPublished 27 October 20264 min read

Militaria sells to the most exacting buyers in collecting. A collector knows the maker's marks, the period variations and the fakes better than most sellers do, and they buy on the description before they buy on the photograph. Selling online is less about finding a platform than about writing listings that survive that reader. We work with an auction house in the United States and a militaria shop in the Netherlands, and the lessons below come from both.

The short version

Pick the channel by the value of the piece: auction for rare items, your own shop for steady stock, marketplaces only where their rules allow the category. Write every listing with the maker, the period, the marks, the condition and the provenance, in that order. Price from realised sales, not asking prices. Photograph the marks as carefully as the front. Then build a list of collectors you can write to directly.

Where should you sell militaria online?

ChannelBest forWatch out for
Specialist auction houseRare and high-value pieces with a known marketCommission, consignment timelines, and the catalogue deadline
Your own online shopSteady stock sold between sales, repeat collectorsYou bring the traffic; nobody else does
General marketplacesLower-value pieces and wide reachRestricted-item rules that remove listings without warning
Collector forums and groupsSpecialist items and trusted sellersTrades on reputation; no buyer protection by default

Most dealers end up using two of these. An auction calendar sells the rare pieces at their best price; a shop of your own sells the rest between sales and keeps the collectors who found you at auction.

What makes a militaria listing trustworthy?

Specific facts in a fixed order. A collector scans for the same things every time, and a listing that puts them where the eye expects them reads as a seller who knows the field.

  1. The item, the nation and the period, in the title.
  2. The maker and every mark, with a photograph of each.
  3. The condition, stated plainly, flaws first.
  4. The provenance: where it came from, and what paperwork goes with it.
  5. What you do not know, said outright. A collector trusts a seller who admits a gap more than one who fills it with guesses.

How do you price militaria?

From what comparable pieces actually sold for, not what other sellers are asking. Asking prices on a marketplace tell you what people hope for; realised prices at auction tell you what collectors paid. If you have your own sales history, it is the best reference you own. The auction house we work with loaded its past items into the system that now drafts its listings, so every new lot is written and estimated against its own record.

How do you write listings faster without losing accuracy?

Write from photographs and your own history, and review only what is uncertain. For the US auction house, every sale meant 1,250 lots typed by hand. We built a platform that reads the photographs, drafts each listing from a 32,000-item reference set and flags only the lots it is unsure of for a person to check. It went live for the auction of 16 August and has taken 400+ hours off the client's desk. For the shop in the Netherlands, an AI writer drafts each product listing and the dealer corrects and publishes it.

What changes between the US, the Netherlands and the rest of Europe?

  • In the United States, the collector market is large and auction-led, and most bidding platforms take a catalogue upload in their own template.
  • In the Netherlands and elsewhere in Europe, some countries restrict the public display or sale of certain symbols and insignia. Check the law where you sell and where you ship before you list.
  • Shipping out of the European Union, or into it from the United States, means customs paperwork. Say in the listing who pays duties.
  • Whatever the country, marketplaces apply their own restricted-item rules on top of the law, and they change them without notice.

When is hiring help the wrong call?

When your stock is small enough to describe yourself in an evening. A writing system pays when the typing is the bottleneck: hundreds of lots a sale, or a back room of photographed stock waiting for words. A dealer with a dozen pieces a month should write them personally, build a list of collectors, and spend the money on better photographs.
