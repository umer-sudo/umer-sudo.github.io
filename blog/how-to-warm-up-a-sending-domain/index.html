How to warm up a sending domain before cold outreach

Why a brand-new domain cannot send cold email on its first day, what a warm-up actually does, and the slow, boring schedule that keeps a domain out of spam.

By Umer Tariq, founderPublished 30 October 2026Updated 17 November 20266 min read

A new domain has no reputation, and to an inbox provider no reputation looks a lot like bad reputation. Spammers register fresh domains every day, burn them in a week and move on. So when a domain registered on Monday sends a few hundred cold emails on Tuesday, it looks exactly like them. Warming up is the process of looking like something else.

The short version

Warming up a sending domain means building its reputation slowly before cold outreach. Set SPF, DKIM and DMARC first, then send a small number of real, plain-text emails a day that get opened and answered, and raise the number gradually over several weeks. Rushing it is the most common way a new outreach domain ends up in spam.

Why does a new domain need warming at all?

Gmail, Outlook and Yahoo judge a sender largely by history: how much it sends, how steadily, and how recipients react. A domain with no history gives them nothing to go on, so they are cautious with it. Every message you send in the first weeks is evidence. Opens, replies and messages rescued from the spam folder count for you. Bounces, deletions without opening and spam reports count against you.

The goal of a warm-up is to build a short history that looks like a real person starting to use a real mailbox: a few messages, mostly to people who answer, growing as the conversations grow.

What has to be in place before the first email?

  • The three records: SPF, DKIM and DMARC, all passing. Our piece on why cold email lands in spam, at /blog/why-cold-email-lands-in-spam, covers how to check them.
  • A real mailbox with a real name, a profile photo and a signature, not a no-reply address.
  • A simple website on the domain, or a redirect to your main site, so anyone who checks the domain finds a business.
  • A custom tracking domain in your outreach tool if you track opens or clicks, so your links do not share a reputation with every other customer of that tool.

We bought hellodrifted.com, at https://hellodrifted.com, for exactly this reason: so that our cold outreach builds, and if it goes wrong spends, its own reputation instead of the one driftedmarketing.com carries for our clients. It went through every step on this list before it sent a single cold message.

What does a sensible warm-up schedule look like?

There is no official number, and anyone who quotes one as a rule is guessing. What every inbox provider rewards is the shape: small, steady, rising slowly, with good reactions throughout. The plan below is the shape we follow, expressed in stages rather than counts, because the right count depends on how many mailboxes you run and how your early messages are received.

StageWhat you sendMove on when
First weekA handful a day per mailbox, to people you know who will replyNothing bounces and replies come back
Second and third weeksGradually more each day, still mostly warm contacts, plain text, no linksMessages keep landing in the inbox, not promotions or spam
From the fourth weekThe first small cold sends, mixed in with ongoing warm trafficReply rate holds and no spam complaints arrive
After thatRaise volume slowly; never jump from a quiet day to a big oneKeep watching placement every week

Most outreach tools include an automatic warm-up feature that trades emails between thousands of mailboxes and marks them as important. It helps with volume and is worth switching on, but it is not a substitute for real replies from real people. Treat it as background noise that keeps the mailbox active, not as the reputation itself.

What kills a warm-up?

  • Sending to a bought or scraped list with dead addresses. A bounce tells the provider you do not know who you are writing to.
  • Links, images and attachments in early messages. Plain text from a person looks like a person.
  • The same message sent word for word to everyone. Inbox providers notice identical content at volume.
  • Big jumps. A quiet week followed by a busy day undoes the steadiness you were building.
  • Ignoring replies. A conversation is the best signal there is, and leaving it unanswered wastes it.

How do you know it is working?

Send a test message every few days to inboxes you control on Gmail and Outlook and look at where it lands. Watch your bounce rate and your reply rate in the outreach tool. Register the domain with Google Postmaster Tools, which shows Google's own view of your domain reputation once you send enough mail for it to report. If messages start sliding into spam, stop raising volume, go back a stage, and look at the list and the copy before you look at the settings.

How early should you warm up before Black Friday?

Start weeks before the sale, not days. Black Friday and Cyber Monday are the busiest sending days of the year, inbox providers are at their strictest, and every brand that went quiet since summer suddenly sends to its whole list at once. A domain that has been sending small, steady volumes to people who open and reply will get through. A new domain, or an old one waking up, that jumps straight to sale-week volume is exactly what filters are built to catch.

In practice: finish the records first, begin the slow ramp described above as early in the autumn as you can, send your most engaged subscribers the first sale emails, and only widen to the rest of the list once those land well. If the warm-up started late, send less in sale week rather than more. A smaller send that reaches the inbox earns more than a big one that lands in junk, and it does not leave the domain damaged for December.

When is warming up the wrong thing to spend time on?

If you only send a few personal emails a week from your normal business address, you do not need any of this. Warming up matters when you plan to contact people who have not heard of you, in volume, over months. And if the offer is weak, a perfectly warmed domain will still get no replies. In that case the work is the offer and the first line, not the domain. We write about choosing what to say at /services/email-marketing.

Questions we get asked

How long does it take to warm up a domain?

Plan on several weeks before the first real cold send, and keep warming after that. Slower is safer. A domain burned in a hurry usually has to be replaced, not repaired.

Is it too late to warm up a domain before Black Friday?

Not entirely, but cut the plan to fit. Warm with your most engaged contacts only, keep sale-week volume modest, and leave the cold list until after the sale.

Can I warm up my main domain instead?

You can, but if cold outreach then damages its reputation, your invoices and client emails suffer with it. A separate outreach domain keeps that risk away from the business.

Do warm-up tools work?

They help keep a mailbox active and are worth using. They do not replace real replies, and a provider can tell the difference over time.

Should I buy several domains?

Only if your volume truly needs it. Every domain needs its own records, mailboxes and warm-up, so more domains means more to look after.
