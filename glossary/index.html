■ 02The termsA to Z

Defined, then what it costs you.

The definition first, then what it means for the person paying. Grouped by letter.

A

Agent-readable summary file

A plain text file, usually published at llms.txt on your own site, that tells an AI assistant who you are, what you do, where, what it costs and which pages matter. A fuller version can carry the pages themselves. For a buyer it is cheap, it is yours to write, and it is the one place you control exactly how an assistant describes you. Keep it factual, because anything in it is quotable.

AI search

Search where an assistant reads the web for you and replies in sentences, citing the pages it used. It is replacing a share of the searches that used to produce a list of links. For a buyer it rewards different things from classic search: a direct answer near the top of a page, one consistent description of who you are across the web, and numbers with dates on them. It punishes vagueness and contradiction.

Answer engine

A search tool that writes an answer instead of listing links: an AI assistant, or the summary now shown above ordinary search results. It reads pages, picks the passages it trusts, and names where they came from. For a buyer it is a new front door. Your brand is either quoted in the answer or absent from it, and absence is invisible. Being quoted depends on pages that state facts plainly, not on advertising.

Answer-first page

A page that states the answer to the question it was written for in its first paragraph, then earns the detail underneath. The opposite of the page that opens with a slogan or a scroll of decoration before saying anything. For a buyer it matters twice: a person decides in seconds whether a page is for them, and an assistant answering a question lifts the paragraph that answers it. A page built this way serves both, and it is how every page on this site is written.

Attribution window

The period after someone sees or clicks an ad during which a sale is credited to that ad. Each platform sets its own, and each sets it generously. For a buyer this is the main reason the ad dashboard and the store disagree: the same order can be claimed by Meta, by Google and by the email that went out that morning. Ask which window a report uses before you trust the number in it.

Audience saturation

The point at which a campaign has shown its ads to most of the people it was aimed at, and keeps paying to show them again. Reach stops growing, frequency climbs, and the cost of each result rises for no fault of the creative. For a buyer it is the usual reason a campaign that worked in month one stalls in month three. The fixes are a wider audience, a new audience, or new creative, and the account will tell you which if someone reads it.

B

Brand search

Searches for your business by name, as opposed to searches for the thing you sell. Someone who types your name has already heard of you, from an ad, a post or a friend. For a buyer it is the cheapest traffic there is and the clearest sign that the rest of the marketing is working, because nobody searches for a name they have not met. Watch it grow month by month, and do not let an agency count it as a result of the search work alone.

C

Call to action

The line that tells the reader what to do next: buy, book, message, sign up, call. Every ad, page and email should carry one, and only one. For a buyer the test is whether it names the next step plainly and whether that step is the thing the campaign is measured on. A vague one wastes the attention everything above it earned, and two of them split the reader between choices, which is usually a way of choosing neither.

One page. One job. →Email marketing: the three emails that pay →

Cash on delivery

Payment made to the courier when the parcel arrives rather than online at checkout. It is the default in Pakistan and much of South Asia, where buyers trust the doorstep more than the payment page. For a buyer it means a confirmed order is not yet a sale, because parcels get refused. Count refusals in the result, confirm orders by message before dispatch, and price the refusals into the margin.

Cash-on-delivery return rate

The share of parcels sent on cash on delivery that come back unpaid, because the buyer refused the door, was not home, or had changed their mind before the courier arrived. Each return costs the outbound and the return shipping and ties up stock. For a buyer selling in Pakistan it is the number that decides whether an ad campaign is profitable, and it is invisible in the ad dashboard. Confirm every order in the inbox before it ships, and charge each return to the campaign that produced it.

Checkout rate

The share of people who reached the checkout and completed the order. It is narrower than conversion rate and more useful for a store, because it isolates the last step: shipping cost, payment options, a form that fails on a phone. For a buyer it is the first thing to check on a store that gets visits but few orders. Most of the fixes are cheap and none of them need more ads.

Conversion rate

The share of people who did the thing you wanted after arriving: bought, booked, subscribed, messaged. It turns traffic into a result. For a buyer it is the number that tells you whether the problem is the ad or the page. Cheap traffic and a low rate point to the page or the offer; expensive traffic and a healthy rate point to the targeting. Measure it for each source separately.

Conversions interface

Meta's Ads Manager calls it the Conversions API. Your store or website tells the ad platform directly, from its own server, when someone buys, books or signs up, instead of relying on a tracking script in the visitor's browser. Browsers and phones now block much of that script, so the platform sees fewer of your sales than happened. For a buyer it means the ads are judged on fuller data and the campaign learns from real purchases. Set it up before spend is scaled.

Core web vitals

Google's measures of how a page feels to use: how quickly the main content appears, how soon it responds to a tap, and whether things jump around while it loads. They are measured on real visitors' phones, not in a lab. For a buyer they are a plain proxy for whether your site loses people before it has said anything. A slow store spends its ad budget on visitors who leave.

Cost per acquisition

The ad spend it took, on average, to produce one of the thing you were counting: an order, a lead, a booking. It is the number a campaign is run against. For a buyer it only means something next to what that acquisition is worth, which is why we ask for the margin on the first call. An acquisition that costs more than it earns is a loss that looks like activity.

Cost per click

The average price paid each time someone clicks an ad. It is easy to read and easy to be misled by: a cheap click from the wrong person costs more than an expensive one from a buyer. For a buyer it is a diagnostic rather than a goal. If clicks are cheap and sales are absent, the ad is attracting the wrong people or the page is losing the right ones.

Cost per thousand impressions

What the platform charges to show an ad a thousand times, whether or not anyone acts on it. It is set by auction, so it rises when more advertisers want the same people and in the weeks before holidays. For a buyer it is a price of attention, not a measure of results: a rise is not a failure, and a fall is not a win unless the sales held. Read it beside the cost per result.

Creative fatigue

What happens when the same people have seen the same ad too many times: the results fall while the cost to show it rises. It is the usual reason a campaign that worked stops working. For a buyer it means creative is a running cost, not a launch cost. An account needs new ads on a schedule, which is why our packages state how many a month and why the losers are cut weekly.

Creative testing

Running several versions of an ad at once, each different in one way, and letting the results choose. The hook, the format, the product shown, the offer. For a buyer it is the difference between an agency that learns and one that guesses with your money. The signs it is happening: more than a couple of ads live at a time, a stated reason for each, a weekly note on what was cut and why. The sign it is not: the same two ads running for a quarter.

Customer acquisition cost

Everything it costs to win one new customer, not just the ads: the fee, the creative, the discount, the time. Cost per acquisition is the ad platform's view; this is the business's view. For a buyer it is the honest number, and it is almost always higher than the dashboard suggests. Work it out once with the agency fee included, and you know what a new customer has to spend before you break even.

D

DM-first selling

A way of selling where the sale closes in a conversation, a direct message on Instagram or WhatsApp, rather than at a website checkout. Common wherever the product needs a question answered first: sizing, a name to embroider, a date, a delivery address. For a buyer it changes everything downstream: the ad asks for a message, the campaign optimises toward the inbox, and the result is counted where the money changes hands.

E

Entity consistency

Describing your business the same way everywhere a machine might read it: the same name, the same place, the same line about what you do, on your site, your Google listing, your social profiles and the directories. For a buyer it matters because an answer engine builds one picture of you from many sources, and a contradiction lowers its confidence in all of them. Choose one sentence about yourself and use it without variation.

F

Frequency

How many times, on average, one person has seen your ad over a period. A low number means you are reaching new people; a high one means you are paying to be ignored by the same ones. For a buyer it is the early warning for creative fatigue. Read it with reach: if reach has stalled and frequency is climbing, the audience is exhausted and the ad needs replacing before the budget does.

G

Google Business Profile

The free listing that appears when someone searches for a local business on Google or Maps: hours, address, photographs, reviews, posts and the button to call or get directions. For a business with a door, it is the page most customers will see before they see the website. For a buyer the work is keeping it true and alive: correct hours, recent photographs, replies to every review, a post a month. A listing that has gone quiet reads as a business that has closed.

H

Hold rate

The share of a video that people watched on average, or the share who stayed to the end. Where hook rate judges the opening, hold rate judges the middle. For a buyer it tells you whether the content earned its length. A video that loses half its viewers by the midpoint is too long, or says its one thing too late. Cut until the hold climbs, then make the next one shorter.

Hook

The first second of a video, or the first line of a post: the part that decides whether anyone stays for the rest. On a feed, the viewer has not chosen to watch; the hook has to earn that choice before the thumb moves. For a buyer it is where most of the creative budget is won or lost, because a strong message behind a weak opening is a message nobody saw. Spend the testing budget on openings, and never open on a logo.

Hook rate

The share of people who kept watching a video past its first moments rather than scrolling on. It measures the opening: the first frame, the first line, the first movement. For a buyer it tells you whether a video got a chance at all. A low hook rate under a strong message means the message was never seen. Fix the opening before you judge the rest, and test openings more than endings.

I

Impressions

The number of times your content or ad was shown, counting every repeat. Impressions are always higher than reach, and a platform is happy to report them because they grow fast. For a buyer they are the denominator for other numbers, such as cost per thousand, rather than a result in themselves. A report that leads with impressions and never gets to sales is a report written to look busy.

L

Landing page

The page an ad sends people to. It has one job, which is to continue the promise the ad made and lead to one action: buy, book, message, sign up. For a buyer it is where most ad budgets are quietly lost. A good ad on a slow or cluttered page pays for traffic that leaves. Before scaling spend, check that the page loads fast on a phone and asks for one thing.

Landing-page view

Counted when a person who clicked an ad actually arrives on the page and it loads, as opposed to a click, which is counted the moment the ad is tapped. The gap between the two is the people who gave up while the page was loading. For a buyer it is the more honest of the two numbers and the quicker diagnosis: a wide gap means a slow page, and no amount of ad spend fixes that.

Learning phase

The period after an ad set is launched or changed, during which the platform is still working out who responds to it. Results in this window swing widely and cost more than they will later. For a buyer it is the reason not to judge a campaign in its first days, and the reason an agency that edits budgets daily never leaves it. Make a change, then leave it alone long enough to learn something.

Lifetime value

What a customer spends with you over the whole relationship, not just the first order. A brand whose customers return can afford to pay more to win each one; a brand selling a one-off purchase cannot. For a buyer it sets the ceiling on acquisition cost. If you do not know it, estimate it from the repeat rate and the average order, and treat the estimate as a guess until the data catches up.

Lookalike audience

An audience the platform builds by finding people who resemble a list you give it: your customers, your subscribers, the people who messaged you. The better the list, the better the match. For a buyer it is how a campaign finds new buyers without guessing at interests. It needs a clean source list of real customers, which means your list hygiene and your privacy notice matter before the first ad runs.

M

Media buying

Placing and managing paid ads on platforms such as Meta and Google: choosing the audiences, setting budgets, running the auction and reading the results. It is distinct from making the ads, though the two are usually sold together. For a buyer the point to settle is how the buyer is paid. A flat fee keeps the incentive on results; a share of spend rewards spending more. The money itself should stay in your own account.

O

Organic reach

The people your content reached without paying to promote it. The platform showed it because people watched, shared or saved it, and decided others would too. For a buyer it is the test of whether content is good, as opposed to merely funded. Paid reach proves you have a budget. Organic reach proves you have something people want to see, and it is the reach that keeps going when the spend stops.

R

Reach

The number of different people who saw your content or ad at least once. It is a count of people, not of views, so one person watching five times counts once. For a buyer it is the honest measure of how far a post or a campaign travelled, and the one to watch when a brand is small. Followers tell you who signed up once; reach tells you who you are in front of this week.

Retainer

A fixed monthly fee for an agreed scope of work, paid whether the month is busy or quiet. It buys continuity: the same people, a running knowledge of your account, and a plan that spans more than one month. For a buyer the terms matter more than the figure. Ask what is included, what is not, the notice period, and who keeps the accounts and the work when it ends. A retainer should be losable by the agency, not by you.

Retainer versus percentage of spend

The two ways an ads agency gets paid. A retainer is a flat monthly fee for an agreed scope; a percentage of spend grows as your ad budget grows. For a buyer the difference is the incentive. On a percentage, the agency earns more when you spend more, whether or not the extra spend returns anything. On a flat fee, a bigger budget is never the agency's idea. Ask which one you are being quoted, and whether the spend sits in your account or theirs.

Retargeting

Showing ads to people who have already met the brand: visited the site, watched a video, opened a message. They are cheaper to convince because they are no longer strangers. For a buyer it is the most efficient spend in the account and the easiest to overstate, because many of those people would have bought anyway. Keep it as a share of the budget, not the whole of it, and judge it on new customers won.

Return on ad spend

The money that comes back for every unit of money put into ads, measured over a stated period. If a campaign brings in five dollars of sales for each dollar spent, the return is five to one. For a buyer it is the number that decides whether ads are a cost or an engine. Ask for it per campaign and with its dates, and ask what counted as the sale: a checkout, a message, or an order confirmed later.

S

Saves

The number of people who saved a post to come back to it. A save is a stronger signal than a like because it costs the viewer something later: a decision to return. For a buyer it is the metric that predicts whether content sells, because people save what they intend to buy, make, visit or send to someone. A post with many saves and few likes is doing its job quietly.

Scope of work

The written list of what an agency will deliver, how much of it, how often, and what is excluded. It is the part of a contract that decides most disputes. For a buyer the test is whether you could check it at the end of a month: eight videos is a scope; ongoing content support is not. Read the exclusions as carefully as the inclusions, since that is where the extra invoices come from.

Search intent

What a person wants when they type a query: to learn, to compare, to find a specific site, or to buy. The same words can carry different intents, and a page that answers the wrong one does not rank or sell. For a buyer it is the difference between traffic and customers. A page for a buying query shows the price and the path; a page for a learning query teaches and earns the next visit.

Structured data

A block of labelled facts in a page's code that a machine can read without guessing: this is an organisation, here is its founder, this is an article with this date, these are the questions and the answers. The reader never sees it; search engines and answer engines do. For a buyer the rule is that it must only say what the page already says. Markup that claims more than the page is a lie a machine will quote.
